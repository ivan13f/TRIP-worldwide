00 · Principles

Two colours, one meaning each

Uneven Ground is an article, not a dashboard. The system is deliberately small: a page of white paper and black ink, one blue that means rights in law, and one vermilion that means look here, this didn't move. Everything else is type and space.

1 · Colour is a sentence

Blue always means score or gain. Vermilion always means the bottom quarter, a loss, or the 2012 break. A reader who learns this in Fig. 1 can read Fig. 4 without a legend.

2 · Prose carries the argument

Every claim lives in the body text. Margin notes, tooltips and annotations add depth but are never required to follow the story.

3 · Data is the only loud thing

Flat colour blocks belong to the hero. Inside a chart, gridlines are hairlines, text is ink and the only saturated ink is data.
01 · Colour

Ten values, three jobs

The four charts need exactly three kinds of colour: a sequential scale for score (map, heatmap), one series pair for "rose" against "stayed / fell" (bands, dot plot), and neutrals for everything that isn't data. No categorical palette exists because no chart needs one. Regions and indicator families are told apart by position and labels, not hue.

The palest step is 2.06:1 against white, so a country scoring 0% is still visible on the map and in the heatmap. Missing data is never a ramp colour; it gets --nodata plus a 45° hatch. TRIP is a count from 0 to 13, so legends show 14 discrete swatches, not a smooth gradient that implies values that don't exist.

Rules

DoUse vermilion for one idea per chart. If two things are vermilion, they must mean the same thing.
Don'tColour text with a data colour. Labels stay ink. Identity comes from the swatch, line key or dot beside the text.
DoUse the same 14-step legend in Fig. 2 and Fig. 4. Same scale, same swatches, same position above the plot.
Don'tAdd a third hue for regions or indicator families. Group with headings and position instead.

04 · Components

Only what the article uses

Ten pieces: hero, act header, body, margin note, stat callout, figure (legend, plot, caption, data table), tooltip, annotation, step card, typology card with carousel, sources. Nothing else gets built.

Act header + body + margin note

Act I · The headline and the trap

The average rose. The floor did not.

Between 2000 and 2021 the world's average TRIP score more than doubled, from 10.9% to 23.5%.

That is the headline most reports would print. It is also misleading. Averages hide distributions, and this distribution came apart: a small group of countries raced ahead while the bottom quarter stayed exactly where it started.

How TRIP is scored

Thirteen yes/no legal indicators, summed and divided by 13. A country with only "no direct criminalization" scores 1 / 13 = 7.7%.

06 · Why it works

Laws, and the decision each one made

Every rule above traces back to a known principle of interface design or graphical perception.

Von Restorff (isolation)The one thing that differs is remembered. Vermilion appears only on the finding (the flat bottom quarter, losses, the 2012 break), so the eye goes there first in every chart.
Gestalt: similaritySame colour = same meaning across all four figures. Fig. 2 and Fig. 4 share an identical 14-step legend in the same position.
Gestalt: proximityLegends sit directly above their plot; direct labels sit at the line ends; margin notes sit 56px from the text, far enough to read as asides and close enough to stay linked.
Cleveland & McGillPosition beats colour for precise comparison. The indicator chart is a dot plot on a shared axis. Colour encodes magnitude only where position is already used for geography or time (map, heatmap).
Data-ink (Tufte)No chart borders, no vertical gridlines, no 3D, no gradients on marks. Gridlines are 1px #D9D9D3. Ink goes to data.
Single-hue sequentialLightness is read as quantity; hue is not. The score scale is one blue, light to dark, validated monotone, and it stays readable in greyscale and for all colour-vision types.
Fitts's lawHover targets are whole rows, columns or 28px circles, never 3px cells. Buttons are 44px; carousel dots have 28 × 44px hit areas.
Hick's lawOne interaction per chart (hover), no filters, no toggles in the article. The fewer choices, the faster the reading.
Doherty thresholdTooltips appear in 100ms with no delay; nothing a reader triggers waits longer than 400ms.
Jakob's lawLinks look like links (underlined), carousels look like carousels (arrows + dots), data tables sit behind a familiar "View the data" disclosure.
Accessible by defaultText never wears a data colour; all text clears WCAG AA; every chart has an aria label and a data table; annotations open on keyboard focus; reduced motion shows the final state.
