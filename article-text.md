## HERO

**Title:** Uneven Ground

**Intro:** Political scientist Myles Williamson's Trans Rights Indicator Project records what the law says about trans people in 173 countries, every year from 2000 to 2021. The average score doubled. The bottom quarter did not move.

**Cue:** Scroll for more

## ACT I

**Kicker:** Act I · 2000 to 2021
**Headline:** The headline and the trap

### Main text

Williamson's TRIP score asks thirteen yes-or-no questions of every country's laws, every year. They fall into three groups.

- **Criminalization, two questions.** Is there no law that directly targets trans people, and none that is used against them indirectly?
- **Recognition, five questions.** Can people change the gender marker on their official documents? Can they do it without surgery or other physical requirements, without a psychiatric diagnosis, and without having to divorce? Is there a nonbinary or third gender option?
- **Protection, six questions.** Is there a general anti-discrimination law covering trans people? Specific protection in employment, education, healthcare and housing? Protection written into the constitution?

In 2000, the average country scored 10.9%. By 2021, it scored 23.5%. Progress, the headline says.

### Aside note (left): About the score

Each yes counts as one point. The score is the number of points out of 13, shown as a percentage. It records what the law says, not how it is enforced or how safe daily life is.

### Fig. 1: step cards

1. The headline: the **average** score more than doubled, from 10.9% to 23.5%.
2. The **median** country tells a quieter story. It held one protection until 2014 and two from 2015: from 7.7% to 15.4%.
3. The **middle half** of countries, between the 25th and 75th percentile. Its top edge climbs from 15.4% to 30.8%. Its bottom edge never moves.
4. The **top 10%** of countries scored 15.4% or more in 2000. By 2021 the threshold was 61.5%. This is where the average's rise comes from.
5. And the **bottom quarter**: 7.7% in 2000, 7.7% in 2021. One protection out of thirteen, unchanged for 22 years.

**Legend:** Average · Median · Middle half (25th to 75th percentile) · Top 10% threshold · Bottom quarter (25th percentile)
**Caption:** Fig. 1. Distribution of TRIP scores across countries, 2000 to 2021.
**Source:** Trans Rights Indicator Project (Williamson, 2024). 170 to 173 countries per year.

### Main text

The world did not rise together. A small group at the top pulled the average up while most countries stayed where they started. That is why the average, 23.5%, now sits well above the median, 15.4%.

### Aside note (right): Measuring divergence

The middle half of countries spanned 7.7 percentage points in 2000 and 23.1 in 2021. That distance, the interquartile range, tripled.

## ACT II

**Kicker:** Act II · Where
**Headline:** The map that isn't there

### Main text

If the bottom held still and the top moved, where is the top?

The expected answer, Western Europe and Scandinavia, is only partly right. Geography does not predict the score.

### Aside note (left): Argentina · 2012

Law 26.743, the Gender Identity Law, passed the Argentine Senate 55 votes to 0. It allowed legal gender recognition with no medical, surgical or judicial requirements.

### Fig. 2: step cards

1. Every country, coloured by how many of the thirteen protections its laws held in 2021. Two thirds of countries hold two or fewer.
2. **South America.** Argentina passed the world's first self-ID law in 2012, yet scores 6 of 13: it has none of the anti-discrimination protections. Uruguay holds 12 of 13, the highest score anywhere.
3. **South Asia.** Pakistan scores 11 of 13, the highest in Asia. India holds 8 of 13: broad protections, but changing a legal gender still requires medical steps.
4. **Europe** is not one place. Malta writes protection into its constitution. Austria, strong on LGB rights, holds 3 of 13. Hungary ended legal gender change in 2020.
5. **Sub-Saharan Africa.** Three in four countries score 0 or 1. Nigeria directly criminalizes trans people.

### Main text

Many of the countries with the most restrictive laws share something beyond their politics: their criminal codes were written under British rule. Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, drafted in 1860, criminalised "carnal intercourse against the order of nature." It, or close copies of it, spread to Pakistan, Bangladesh, Malaysia and Singapore, and similar "unnatural offences" clauses entered the codes of Nigeria, Kenya and Uganda. Courts and police have applied such clauses to gender-nonconforming people for over a century.

Iran presents a different anomaly. The state has funded gender-affirming surgery since the 1980s, yet same-sex relations remain punishable by death. On TRIP it scores 2 of 13: legal gender change exists, but only after surgery, a psychiatric diagnosis and divorce.

### Aside note (right): Section 377 · 1860

India's Supreme Court struck the clause down for consensual adults in 2018. Versions of it remain in force elsewhere in the former empire.

## ACT III

**Kicker:** Act III · What
**Headline:** What changed, and what didn't

### Main text

The geography tells us where progress happened. The thirteen indicators tell us what kind.

Not all of them moved, and one moved backward.

### Aside note (left, beside Fig. 3): The one that went backwards

Eight countries criminalized trans people directly in 2000, twelve in 2021. Five added such laws, including newly independent South Sudan; Guyana removed its law in 2018.

**Fig. 3 legend:** 2000 · 2021, rose · 2021, fell
**Caption:** Fig. 3. Share of countries with each protection in law, 2000 and 2021.
**Source:** TRIP (Williamson, 2024). Author's calculations.

### Main text

Gender marker change led the improvement, from 11% of countries in 2000 to 35% in 2021. Protection in employment followed, reaching 20%. But constitutional protection exists in just 3% of countries, and a nonbinary option in 5%. Direct criminalization is the only indicator that moved backwards.

### Aside note (right): LGB vs. trans

Austria scores 82% for LGB protections (ILGA World, 2020) but 23% on TRIP. The indicators measure different rights for different groups.

### Country cards (carousel)

**Argentina**: Recognition without protection · 6 of 13 · 46% · 2021
In 2012, the first country to let people change their legal gender by declaration alone, with no medical requirements. A nonbinary marker followed in 2021. Yet none of the six anti-discrimination protections is in law.

**India**: Protection with gatekeeping · 8 of 13 · 62% · 2021
Legal gender change and a third gender option since 2014, and anti-discrimination protection in employment, education, healthcare and housing. But changing a legal gender still requires medical steps.

**Austria**: The progressive exception · 3 of 13 · 23% · 2021
Scores 82% on LGB rights but 23% on trans rights. Legal gender change is possible, yet none of the six anti-discrimination protections covers trans people.

**Pakistan**: Courts moved before laws did · 11 of 13 · 85% · 2021
A 2009 Supreme Court ruling recognized a third gender. The 2018 Transgender Persons Act added self-identification and protection in employment, education, healthcare and housing. Same-sex relations remain criminalized.

**Iran**: The medical exception · 2 of 13 · 15% · 2021
State-funded gender-affirming surgery since the 1980s, but legal gender change requires surgery, a psychiatric diagnosis and divorce. Same-sex relations remain punishable by death.

**United States**: No federal recognition · 5 of 13 · 38% · 2021
There is no federal way to change a legal gender marker; that is left to the states. Its score comes from federal anti-discrimination protection in employment, education, healthcare and housing.

**Kicker:** Act IV · Every country, every year

### Main text

What does the complete record look like?

173 rows, one per country, sorted by their 2021 score from highest at the top to lowest at the bottom. 22 columns, one per year. Each cell is coloured by how many protections that country held that year.

### Fig. 4: step cards

1. Each row is a country, each column a year. Hover a row, or search for a country.

3. Through the 2000s, a small group began to move. The rest stayed still.
4. After **2012**, the year Argentina enacted self-determined gender recognition, more countries followed and the gap became structural.

## EPILOGUE

**Kicker:** Epilogue
**Headline:** Law is not life

### Main text

These are laws. They do not record what happened in the streets, in hospitals, in families. A country can score 85% and still be dangerous. A low score can coexist with strong community networks that laws do not see. The legal environment matters. It shapes what police do, what doctors can say, what documents a person can hold. But it is not the whole story.

This data ends in 2021. Since then, at least 27 US states have enacted anti-trans legislation. Uganda's Anti-Homosexuality Act of 2023 introduced life imprisonment provisions affecting trans people. Hungary and Russia have expanded restrictions. The progress you saw is not guaranteed. Some of it is already being undone.

### About this data

This piece uses the Trans Rights Indicator Project (TRIP), a dataset of 13 legal indicators for 173 countries from 2000 to 2021, developed by Myles Williamson as part of his doctoral research at the University of Alabama and published in Perspectives on Politics (Cambridge University Press), Vol. 22, No. 3 (2024). All scores, shares and counts in this article are calculated from the TRIP data. The LGB comparison uses ILGA World's State-Sponsored Homophobia Report (2020). Post-2021 references: ACLU Legislative Tracker, Human Rights Watch.
